Working design page

The design system for Aluna as a web desktop: the window, the line, the palette and the type.

A capability in Aluna is an app on a desk. It has a logo on the ground and a window when you open it — one window, which is also the whole content area: a collection, a record, a confirmation and the narration of a build all land in the same frame.

The line those windows are drawn with belongs to the whole surface, not to windows alone. Buttons, inputs, cards, tables and status chips all carry a drawn boundary, and every corner you can press is mitred. No CSS border on this page is visible.

How to read it. The desk below is a working implementation of the decisions on this page, not a mock-up of one. Where something is a placeholder, such as the capability fixtures and the timer standing in for a build, the page says so.

One window. The desk holds a single capability window and never a second one. There is no taskbar, so the logos on the ground are the only standing list of what exists. Clicking a logo opens its capability; clicking another swaps what is inside the frame, which does not move and does not redraw. You drag the window by its title bar and resize it from the bottom-right corner. The two lamps maximise it and put it away. There is no third: minimise is not offered, because with no taskbar a minimised window would be hidden exactly as thoroughly as a closed one, and both come back by the same click on the same logo.

That window is the content area. Opening a record replaces the collection inside it and a back control returns you; a build takes it over and streams its narration there; a confirmation lands in it too. Nothing on this surface opens over anything else, which is the whole reason there is no modal in Aluna — a popup that makes the rest of the desk inert to look at one book is exactly what a desktop is supposed to prevent.

The developer panel is the one exception and it is furniture rather than a capability: a second window, read-only, opened from its own tile on the ground, and allowed to sit beside the capability you are watching. Between them, the browser remembers exactly two presentation records — one desktop box and its flags per window — and nothing else. Which capability is open is the address's job.

The desk has edges, and one of them is not an edge. Logos fill down a column and wrap to the next, taking as many columns as the desk is tall and wide, the way every OS desktop behaves — a product whose premise is make as many tools as you want cannot stop at eleven. The prompt bar floats over the bottom of the surface, and the strip it occupies is a floor: no window can be dragged or resized into it, so the tail of a records list is never hidden under the bar and never unclickable. Below 720px the window stops floating and is the screen — no drag, no resize, no maximise — and the logos stay on the ground behind it.

Address / — what the browser's address bar would read (D14). This page cannot own the real one, so it says it here instead.

Why closing is not deleting

Close means put away. The window disappears, the logo stays and the address falls back to the bare desk, because nothing that is true has changed — the window's own box is all that ends with it (D9). Deleting a capability is a separate action, explicit and confirmation-gated, and it is deliberately not reachable from the window chrome. That is why no lamp is signal red. The confirmation it does use fills the window, like everything else.

Where it is reachable from is the logo. A short context menu opens on it carrying Rename and Delete, by right-click, by press-and-hold, or by the keyboard menu key, since the logo is already a real button. On commit the window puts itself away and the tile vanishes, and a link to a capability that is gone loads the bare desk with a brief notice.

Grow one and watch it being made

Type into the prompt bar and press Make it. Two things happen at once. A placeholder tile lands in the logo grid and works away there until the build is done, which is the ambient half — visible whether or not you are looking at the window. And the window opens on the narration straight away, taking over whatever was in it, because there is one window and a build is what it is doing now.

Press Cancel and the window gives back what the build took. The tile has no artwork because there is none yet: the logo is requested last, once the build has cleared its gate, so nothing is paid for a capability that can still be refused. The exact treatment of a working tile is an implementation choice; that it is a placeholder and not a finished logo is not.

Clears the window's box and the developer panel's, which is everything the browser keeps. The capabilities themselves are untouched, because nothing is stored per capability.

Placeholders, named. One is left. The four capabilities on the desk are fixtures, not generated units — their logos are not, though: those are real generations, made to the logo contract and drawn here under its tile and label rules. The wallpaper was a placeholder too, but the artwork now exists and the desk is standing on it.

Why the rule runs through the frame

Every other line in the window is structural. It encloses something or divides something, and it stops where the frame stops. The title-bar rule is the only mark that does neither, and its being the only one is why it reads as deliberate. If the corners crossed as well, crossing would become a pattern and stop carrying meaning.

Why the title bar is cut into panes

Ordered flat fills are the depth model on this surface, so a gradient in the title bar would be the only soft edge in the system. The cuts also leave something open that a gradient would close: five discrete cells can each be lit on their own if the bar ever has to carry live state.

Four rules the implementation has to keep

The noise has to be smooth and periodic, or the frame reads as corroded. The seed has to live on the window and never be derived from its position or size, or a re-render during a drag re-rolls the hand. The path is regenerated on resize only, since dragging is a transform. And the corners have to be pinned, because a spline that runs through a corner rounds it, and mitred quietly becomes a 3px radius.

D10 settled that the line is drawn rather than ruled, and it settled that for the window. D11 extends it to the whole surface. Every visible boundary on this page uses the same line as the window frame: the buttons in this section, the search rail, the record cards, the status chips on them, the specification tables and the stages. Each one deviates from true, is inked twice, is mitred wherever it has a corner, and carries its own seed. No CSS border on this page is visible.

Why there are three hands. When a button's edge wanders as far off true as the window frame around it, the surface reads as noisy, and the window stops sitting in front of its own contents. The fine hand fixes that without introducing a weight ladder: every line on this page is still 2px, and only the amplitude steps down. The wavelength steps down with it, because a long wave on a short edge reads as a bowed rectangle instead of a lighter hand.

The close hand answers the same objection one scale further down. A 22px checkbox under the fine hand runs 2% of its own width off true, against 0.15% for a window, so the fine hand there does not read as lighter. It reads as a dented square. Only the amplitude moves again, because at 42px the wavelength is already short enough to complete cycles on a part that small.

Re-ink redraws every boundary on the page at once with a fresh hand. The treatment does not change when you press it, since the three hands, the 2px weight and the amplitudes are all fixed, and yet no two results are the same. There is no switch back to ruled borders and no slider on the deviation: drawn is settled, and both hands were transcribed from the plate books instead of being chosen on this page.

Why a CSS border cannot do this

A border cannot deviate 0.9px from true, it cannot be inked twice, and its shadow cannot follow a deviation it does not have. The shadow decides it. An edge reads as drawn when the ink and everything touching it deviate together, and it reads as a crooked photocopy when they do not. So a bounded element here is drawn instead of styled, from two SVG layers sandwiching the real content, with ground behind and ink in front.

What CSS keeps

The background, and every colour in it. A 2px stroke centred on the path covers ±1px, and the largest deviation on the surface is 0.9px, so a true rectangle of background can never escape from under the drawn line. That is why the desk above can carry a wallpaper under a drawn window, and why a new button variant needs to know nothing about any of this. A shadow gets no such cover, because it lands outside the ink where a straight edge next to a drawn one shows. So a shadow is a path too: the silhouette displaced, then cut back to the crescent that falls clear of the element.

What decides whether a part is drawn

The test is the perimeter, not the edge. A drawn boundary runs as a closed ring, so what has to be long enough to complete a cycle of the deviation is the whole way round rather than any one side. A 22px checkbox has 88px of perimeter and a 20px status chip has 238px, and both are drawn, where a 20px straight edge on its own reads as bent rather than as drawn. Moving a part between drawn and ruled is one line in ink.js, and this is the question that line answers.

A shadow is cast onto the desk

Only the things that sit on the desk have one: the window, the prompt rail and the logo tile. Nothing inside a window casts a shadow, including buttons, cards, notes, tables and this note. A shadow on a component claims it floats above the paper it is printed on, and on a flat surface with no gradients and no blur that claim reads as grime. Depth here comes from ordered flat bands first and from the hands second.

What squaring the corners cost

One locked line. The collection header's full-width pill input is now square and drawn. The pill was protecting the idea that a soft corner marks the things you type into, and one edge treatment across the whole surface turned out to be worth more than that. The larger cost was the predicted one: the change reached into the shared button, field and card primitives instead of stopping at window chrome. That work is done.

Flat fills at high chroma, with no shading. Depth comes from ordered bands, light in front and dark behind, and never from a gradient or a blur. How muted a colour is depends on how much area it covers: a colour spread across a large area is held back, and the same colour in a small area runs at full strength.

Tint anchors: the defaults Aluna reaches for first

Eight named colours, and the list is closed. A capability's ground colour is one of these eight, named by the model that writes the spec: telescope on sky, recipes on ochre. Signal is not offered. Nothing measures chroma or lightness afterwards, because all eight were chosen saturated and light enough for daylight and there is nowhere else to go — checking the name against the list is the whole of the validation.

Reserved

Which colours can carry a label

The fill names the role, and the label on it is ink unless the fill is too dark to carry ink. Only shade and signal are, so only those two take a light label. Ink clears 4.5:1 on every other anchor except teal at 4.34 and violet at 4.38, and that is why neither of those two is a button: they stay colours a capability wears. Every pairing on a button passes. The one that did not was a light label on leaf at 3.01, and it is gone: the two greens changed places, so shade is the primary at 5.18 and leaf is the secondary at 4.54, taking ink like every other light anchor. See C12.

Violet is the focus ring, and it can be precisely because it is the anchor with no other job. Carrying no readable text at either end, it was never going to be a button or a fill behind a label, which is what leaves it free to mean one thing.

One ink, used for lines and type only. #20301C at 2px around shapes. It is never a background, never a card and never a fill, and any large dark shape uses a saturated hue instead. There is also no dark theme. This palette is daylight and does not invert, so the viewer's OS preference is ignored in both directions. That was decided against, not deferred.

Two variable faces, both OFL, each self-hosted as a single woff2 with no CDN and no build step. No monospace face appears anywhere in the product surface. That holds on this page too: every value set as code is Outfit with tabular figures.

Layout and type are relative. Body size, the window title, the small-caps size, the label measure, the grid track, the window minimum, gaps and paddings are all set in rem, so browser text scaling grows the box along with the text it holds — the most-used accessibility setting there is. The pixel sizes named on these pages are what those come to at the browser's default. Drawing constants stay in pixels: the ink line's weight and its deviation, the logo tile's box and its contour, the 10% corner clip. Those describe a picture rather than a layout, and scaling them changes the artwork's character instead of the layout's fit.

The collection header · feed

Search comes first at full width, so it never competes for space on its row. Below it the count and the create action share one line, which puts the two collection-level facts together. Type in the search box and the count updates live. A filtered count is more useful than a total, and it doubles as feedback for the search without needing a separate status line. The filtering itself is a fixture shortcut, because this page has no server: in the product the search is server-side and debounced, and the count arrives with the records.

The same header · grid

The header is unchanged. Only row 3 differs, following the capability's declared layout, and feed and grid are the closed set of layouts.

The record form

The field label sits in small caps above its control, the guidance below it in subtle ink, and related short fields are paired two-up. A rule separates the actions row, which carries save and cancel on the left and keeps the destructive action on the right, away from them. That separation carries "close means put away" down to the record level: a destructive action is never next to a routine one. A record being created has no Delete at all, because there is nothing yet to destroy.

The form is shown here on its own so the field treatment can be read at rest. In a window it always arrives under a back control, reached either from a record or from New record.

These are settled, and everything above is built to them. They are constraints on implementation, not options.

What D8 commits to, and what it only honours

Contrast is the commitment. The palette is closed, so every colour pair is measured once and stays measured, and every pair a button uses passes — see High Meadow and C12. Everything else under D8 is honoured without being a release gate.

Motion is on by default and is part of the product's personality. When the OS Reduce Motion setting is on, Aluna stops positional travel — windows flying open, content sliding, press-jumps — because travel is what triggers nausea. In-place character carries on: the companion keeps breathing, blinking and reacting. Travel is switched off centrally on that one axis, rather than by a blanket reset or a list of components maintained by hand.

The focus ring is one colour and two rules. It is violet, painted on the enclosing shell with the inner control's own ring suppressed. A text input shows it on any focus, mouse click included, because the ring tells you where typing will land and that is real information; every other control shows it on keyboard focus only.

Consequences that bind implementation

D4 makes the logo load-bearing, since with no taskbar it is both the capability's permanent identity and the only way back to a window you closed. D12 falls out of the same place: a third lamp would need somewhere to put a window that is neither on the desk nor gone, and there is no such place. D3 means every title-bar control has to be reversible, so no destructive affordance can appear in window chrome.

D1 and D2 together are what let almost everything else stay small. One window means one swap target, so nothing has to be re-addressed per window; one focused thing, so the prompt is never ambiguous about what it is talking to; one reader per capability, so a deletion drain has one surface to wait on rather than several. D14 falls out of the same fact: a single path can describe the whole desk only because there is only ever one thing in it.

What D2 costs is restoration. A build takes the window over, so the window has to be able to give back what the build displaced when it is cancelled, fails, goes stale or comes back a no-op. That path is alive rather than vestigial, and it is the price of the window being the content area.

D9 keeps nothing per capability, so nothing has to be purged when one is deleted or reconciled against the registry at load — the two cases a layout table would have owed an answer for. D10's hand is rolled at open and never stored, so there is nothing left for a seed to be derived from position or size and re-roll on. D11 extends that to everything else: every drawn boundary carries its own seed, and no seed may come from where the element happens to sit. A record card seeds from the record's own id, so one record keeps its hand across a view swap and across a resize, and the generation pipeline never learns the ink system exists. Resize is watched once per list rather than once per card, because the children of a list resize together.

Question 1 is closed. It asked whether components should follow the frame. The answer was yes, and it went further than the question did: they follow the line as well as the corner. It is D11 now, it lives in The line, and both locked lines it contradicted have been paid.

What dropping the weight ladder cost, and what paid for it

With every line at 2px, the window frame and a button edge were the same line, and a window never quite sat in front of its contents. The fix on the table was a weight ladder after all, at 3px for the frame and 2px for everything else. D11 paid for it another way: the frame and the button are both still 2px, and the frame deviates 0.9px where the button deviates 0.45px, so the hierarchy lives in the hand.

A hand turned out to be more than an amplitude. The model was tuned on windows and cards, which are large in both directions, and it spread its waves evenly around the perimeter. On a control that is 220px one way and 36px the other, the two short edges share 14% of that perimeter between them, so each saw less than one wave. They did not bend; they slid, perfectly straight and slightly offset, which reads as a printing error. Each edge now gets its own budget, floored so a short edge still bows once.

The budget was capped too, and the cap made the same mistake at the other end of the scale. A cap stretches a long edge instead of calming it: six waves across this note is one bow every 130px where the wavelength asks for 42, and at 0.45px the result is a straight line missing true by a third of its own stroke width. The window holding this note is wider than the note and has always read as drawn, because it inks its edges at length / wavelength with no cap, giving a bow every 58px at any size. The cap is gone, no amplitude moved, and the note you are reading is drawn at the rate it was always specified at.
