The control set · working design page

The controls a generated form can reach for, and how each one is drawn.

The desk page settled the treatment: mitred, drawn, inked twice and flat. This page settles the set. It holds every control a generated form can use, in each state it can be in, plus the two that did not exist yet.

Everything here is real, so you can type into the fields, open the listboxes, tick the boxes and watch the long-text field grow. A control that only looks right in a screenshot is what this page is meant to catch.

Three assumptions from the old surface no longer hold. There is no radius, and no radius token to reach for. There is no shadow on a button, a field or an open dropdown. And ink is the only border colour. That last one has consequences: a field cannot turn its boundary red to say it is wrong, so the error state had to be carried somewhere else.

Redraws every boundary on the page with a fresh hand. The treatment does not change and no two results are the same.

A drawn boundary is two SVG layers sandwiching real content, so whatever carries one has to be able to have children. <input> is a void element, and <select> admits only <option>. Neither can hold a layer, so neither can be drawn. Every control below is shaped around that.

Every control is a shell plus a bare native element

The shell is an ordinary span, and it carries the boundary, the fill and the padding. The real input, textarea or select sits inside it with no border and no background of its own. The search rail already worked this way before it was named as a pattern.

The shell buys two more things beyond the line. Focus, hover, disabled and invalid all live on it, so each state is declared once and every control inherits it. And an affordance such as a chevron, a unit or a counter sits inside the boundary instead of beside it.

Why the shadow question never comes up here

A shadow is cast onto the desk, and every control on this page lives inside a window. So the open dropdown further down does not float on a shadow. It reads as being in front because it is drawn in the frame hand over the lightest fill on the palette. Depth here is ordered bands first and hands second, and never a blur.

Why an invalid field keeps its black line

There is one ink, and the drawn line uses it. A boundary that changed colour would be a second ink, which the palette does not allow. So invalid is carried by the well, which takes a wash of signal at the strength a flat surface can hold, and by the guidance, which switches from advice to the reason for the error. The division also does more work: an outline says that something is wrong, and a sentence says what.

Two rules generate the whole spectrum. The fill names the role, and it comes from the tint anchors and the surface fill, never from a new hue or a shade of an existing one. The text is ink unless the fill is too dark to carry it, which on this palette is true of exactly two fills.

Seven variants and a default. outline is the only unfilled one, for the action you take when you are not taking the action, and it is what ghost was called before the set was settled. There is no neutral: it filled with ground-deep, which is the desk's band colour, and a button standing inside a window has no business wearing it.

Why teal and violet are not buttons

Ink clears 4.5:1 on every tint anchor except those two, landing at 4.34 on teal and 4.38 on violet. Neither is dark enough to carry surface either, at 3.16 and 3.12. No text colour works on them, so they stay what they already were: colours a capability wears on a logo band or a chip, where nothing has to be read off them at 10.5px.

Sizes

Three heights and one line weight. The 2px does not scale with the button, because it is the same pen used on a smaller object. A field and a button share the middle height, so a control row aligns without a nudge, and btn--block takes a button full width.

States

Hover steps the fill 13% toward ink. It is derived, not declared, so a new variant costs one line and there is no second palette of hover colours to keep in step. Press displaces the button 2px into the paper. That used to be a move onto the button's own shadow, and it still reads as a press without one.

Disabled prints the whole object lighter, with line, fill and text together at 42%. Greying the boundary on its own would make it a second ink.

One shell holds six field types, and the states are declared on the shell, not on any of them. The label sits in small caps above the control and the guidance below it in subtle ink, so the label is read before the control it names.

Guidance is a key on the field, and there is no placeholder key. A hint written into the control disappears the moment you start typing, which is when a format hint matters; guidance survives it. Guidance also carries the sentence announcing a default, so a default needs no key of its own. When the field is wrong, the error takes the guidance's place — one slot, one voice, and nothing else on the field moves.

The states

Optional is marked, and required is not

The usual way round puts an asterisk on most of the fields on screen to say the unremarkable thing, and puts it in signal, a colour reserved for something having gone wrong. Marking the exception instead costs one quiet word and leaves signal reserved for errors. The spec already knows which fields are required, so this costs nothing but the renderer, and so do read-only and disabled.

Who writes the words in an error

Not the generated form. Showing an error under the field it belongs to is presentation and the platform's; the sentence for a business rule the capability declared is content and belongs to that declaration. A form that wrote its own copy for a declared error would put two versions of the same rule on screen, in two voices, out of step from the first evolution onwards.

The browser also checks required fields before submitting. That recovers the native constraint validation a drawn picker gives up, in the same place and the same style, with no round-trip.

New, and settled. Aluna has no long-text control today. A field holding three sentences gets the same single-line input a title does, and the text scrolls sideways past a caret you cannot follow. It is a key on the form's declared intent, beside the list-input modes, and it fixes every notes, description, review and journal field there will ever be.

Growing

There is no resize grip. A textarea's own grip is three diagonal lines drawn by the operating system in the bottom-right corner. It cannot be styled or drawn, and it would be the only mark on this page that is not ours. Losing it also improves the behaviour, since dragging a box to fit text you have already written is work the box can do on its own.

With a limit

The count lives in the guidance slot, which is where a field already says things about itself. It turns signal only once the limit is passed, because a counter that goes red for the last twenty characters trains you to ignore it. The limit is declared once on the field and drives both this counter and the handler's validation, so the number you are counting down to and the number the server enforces cannot drift apart.

What decides between an input and a textarea

The field type cannot decide it, since both hold a string. It is a presentation choice, so it belongs in the capability's declared intent beside collection.layout and item.shows and not in its schema. A form.long_text list naming the fields that get one has the same shape as the form.list_inputs list that already picks between a comma-separated and a repeatable list input, which is why that is the shape it takes.

New, and settled. A closed set of values has no control at all today. An enum is stored as a string and typed by hand into a text field, which leaves a validation rule doing the work a control should do. This is the second missing control, and the only one on the surface that a native element cannot carry.

It becomes a field type of its own, a scalar that carries its own declared values, with the full picker below it: options in groups, a note under an option, and an option that is there but cannot be chosen. Which control it renders as — picker, radio group or segmented — is declared per field in the spec, and never inferred from how many options there happen to be.

Theirs · a native select

Open them both, one after the other

Closed, the two are indistinguishable. The shell pattern gets the native one exactly as far as the drawn boundary and no further. Open, one is this surface and the other is macOS.

A <select>'s popup is drawn by the browser, entirely outside the stylesheet's reach. It arrives rounded, shadowed, blue-highlighted and set in the system face. On a surface where every boundary is drawn, that popup is visibly foreign.

What the panel does instead of a shadow

Nothing inside a window casts a shadow, and a dropdown is inside a window. So the panel comes forward using two things the language already has. It is drawn in the frame hand, at the window's amplitude instead of a component's, and it is filled with surface, the lightest fill, over the surface-2 well it covers. Light in front, dark behind, and the heavier hand on the thing that holds.

What it costs

About 300 well-trodden lines: the ARIA select-only combobox pattern, arrow keys, typeahead, click-outside handling, and a flip when there is no room below. In exchange, an enum stops being a text field with a rule attached, and the control generation reaches for most often no longer breaks the surface.

A boolean field, and the two choice controls that show every option at once instead of opening. The real input is still the thing that is focused, checked and submitted, and the mark beside it is what gets drawn, for the same reason every other control has a shell.

Which of the three a choice field uses is declared on the field, not inferred from the number of options. Four statuses might be a picker in one capability and a radio group in another, and the capability is what knows whether they are worth the room — a count cannot tell you that, and a rule based on one would keep changing the control under a user every time an option was added.

The third hand, and why these needed one

The marks above are 22px and drawn in the close hand, at 0.28px on a 20px wavelength, against the fine hand's 0.45 on 42. The rule they broke said that parts under about 20px stay ruled, because a short edge never completes a cycle of the deviation. The status chip had already shown that the objection was about perimeter and not height, and a 22px square has 88px of perimeter. That is four cycles at this wavelength, and none at the fine hand's.

So the question was which hand to use, not whether to draw at all. The alternative was a ruled checkbox sitting beside a drawn field, which would leave the last visible CSS border on the surface in the one place it is easiest to compare.

Every control on this page in one form, laid out the way the record form already is: label in small caps above its control, guidance below in subtle ink, related short fields paired two-up, and an actions row separated by a rule with the destructive action kept on the right.

The three questions this page opened are closed. The focus ring is violet, the choice marks get a third hand, and a primary button carries a light label on a fill dark enough to need one, which is shade rather than leaf. They are C10, C11 and C12 now.

So is the question of where a control's shape is declared. Long text is a key on the form's declared intent, shaped like the list-input modes that already sit there. The harder half went the other way, as it had to: an enum's values are a schema change, so a choice field became a type of its own that carries them, and which control it renders as is declared per field beside them (C13).

The date and number pickers are still the browser's

A calendar popup and a spinner, both replaced elements, and both outside the stylesheet in the same way <select> is. A date field with no way to open a picker costs more than a foreign glyph does, so both stay for now and only their colour is claimed.

If the calendar ever has to be ours, it costs the same 300 lines the listbox did, and it also has to know about months.

A list-of-strings field has no picture here

It is a type Aluna already ships, in two input modes — comma-separated and repeatable — and this page draws neither. It gets a drawn control like everything else, and what that control looks like is a present gap rather than a future one.

A file field is the other absence, and it is not a gap. Files do not exist yet; the control arrives with the module that gives them somewhere to live.
