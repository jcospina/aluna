The logo · settled specification

Logo contract

The desk page settled what a logo is: the standing list of what exists, a capability's permanent identity, and the way back to a window you closed. This page settles how one is made. The contract has two halves: a request sent once, as the last step of a build that has already cleared its gate, and a set of things the shell applies to whatever comes back.

Why this is a contract. Generation is asked for exactly two things: a subject and its two colours. The corner, the shadow, the size and the name under it are all applied afterwards, by code that never varies. Whatever the model decides can differ between two logos on the same desk, so the list of what it decides is kept as short as it can be.

Why the tile carries no line

Every other boundary on this surface is drawn, and this one is not. A logo is a picture, and a picture that arrives as a solid colour field already reports where it stops. Ringing it in ink would put a second edge inside the first, which the line does nowhere else. What seats the tile on the desk is the shadow, and it is the surface's own: displaced 3 across and 4 down, never blurred.

Why the corner is the shell's, and rounded

The colour field arrives as a full-bleed 2048² path that touches its own corner, in every case measured. Where the service rounds a corner at all it paints four white wedges over that square instead of cutting a mask into it, so the capability's own colour is already underneath. A clip applied here therefore lands identically on every generation, including the ones that came back square, and the white cannot survive it: the largest wedge measured 175 of 2048, or 8.5%, against a clip of 10%. Probed on the rendered pixels, reading-journal reads 254,254,252 two pixels in from the corner and its own gold, 242,179,44, on the clip line.

What that guarantees is no white, not a uniform corner. The clip reveals whatever the artwork put under there, and a generation that painted something other than its flat ground in the corner still shows it. That is a prompt defect, not a corner one.

It is the only rounded corner on the surface, and it goes against D11. Two things make it the exception: a logo is a picture where everything else here is a boundary, and it has to read as something you can press at 20px.

One call per capability, made once, as the last step of the build — after the gate has cleared and nothing can still refuse the capability (L10). Everything except the colours and the subject is fixed, and no caller may vary it.

Ordering it last is what makes the price safe. A build that fails, goes stale or is cancelled costs nothing, because nothing was ordered; and a capability whose call fails after that is finished, usable, and keeps its placeholder until a retry succeeds (L11).

The model names a hue; Aluna names the shade. The spec authors two of eight hue families — grass green, forest green, teal, cyan, golden yellow, mustard ochre, coral orange, amethyst violet — and they must differ. Each opens onto four shades, and which of the four a capability actually wears is drawn from its incarnation seed. Signal is reserved and is not offered. The model choosing the hue is what keeps the colour apt; the seed choosing the shade is what keeps two capabilities from wearing the same colour, and the check stays a word against a list. Nothing measures chroma or lightness at validation, because all thirty-two rungs were chosen saturated and light enough for daylight.

The shade ladder exists because the model collapses to a mode. The vocabulary was the palette's own token names until 2026-08-25, and the first four live capabilities came out sky, leaf, sky, sky — a notebook, a watering can, a laboratory notebook and a house. Balancing the prompt's worked examples had already been tried and did not touch it: five probe builds against the balanced prompt answered with the same companion three times. A spec model asked for a colour for a notebook gives the same answer every time, and each build is a stateless call that has never seen another capability, so nothing upstream of the seed can produce variety. Two of the eight names — sky and shade — also named things rather than hues, and the ground is defined as what sits behind the object; the house went on sky. The names are hues now, and no worked example names a colour at all. L9 still spends nothing on keeping two capabilities apart: two may still name the same hue, and the ladder is what makes that survivable rather than a rule against it.

A user cannot steer any of this. The subject comes from what the capability is for, never from art direction typed into the prompt bar. A prompt trying to direct a logo is refused where "move this 2px right" is refused, by the intent classifier, under the general rule that presentation is not the user's to set. There is no logo-specific defence, because none is owed one.

One block for every capability. Three slots: the subject, the colour the object is drawn in, and the ground named in words. Nothing else varies, so four logos on one desk read as a set.

A flat colour square of <subject>, drawn in <a flat cool amethyst violet>, on <a flat warm golden yellow> background. One single flat colour fills the whole background from edge to edge — an unbroken field, not panels, not blocks, not stripes, not a checkerboard, no floor, no wall, no horizon, no ground line, no perspective. The object sits centred on that field, large, filling most of the square with an even margin. Bold flat shapes with a confident dark contour, three or four flat colours in total, no gradient, no texture, no shadow. One object seen from one angle. Every surface is blank and unmarked: no text, no letters, no numerals, no labels, no engraving, no writing of any kind. Legible at 64 pixels. The two colours above are given — use them exactly as named. Any further colour stays in the same daylight register at high chroma: no near-blacks, no greys, nothing washed out. Keep the object clearly readable against its background.

The subject slot takes one object described in a phrase: an open book lying flat, one page half-turned, with a ribbon bookmark curling out from the spine. It comes from what the capability actually holds, and it is emitted during spec generation alongside the two colours. Both colours are named in words as well as in the controls, for the reason L2 records about the ground: the control alone is ignored. The measurement behind L2 was made on background_color; the companion is named on the same evidence rather than on its own, and the four specimens on this page predate the second slot.

Six rules the wording keeps

Describe the style, never name it: "ligne claire" returns shaded comic illustration, while describing an even contour and flat fills returns clear line. Say what you want instead of what you don't, since negative lists barely steer this model; the bans that survive are the ones about content, and bans on treatment do not hold. Offer one colour, never a palette: given the whole palette the model reaches for green every time, or blocks the ground into quadrants. Keep spatial words out of the style wording, because they do not stay there — "stacked", written to describe how the shapes sit, leaked into the subject and returned a stack of books for Customers. Never say screenprint, poster or app icon: the first two pull typography into the image, and the third pulls bevels, rounded rectangles and long shadows. And wrap the injected description, or the model letters it into the drawing.

What still gets through, and is kept

Gradients are defined in every generation measured, despite the ban. Corners sometimes arrive with an icon-mask artifact the words did not ask for. Backgrounds occasionally block themselves into panels, which the two-colour rule reduces without eliminating. The corner clip removes the first artifact and the other two are simply kept: a logo is generated once and used as it arrives, so whatever a capability is born with is what it looks like. None of this is open — it is decided.

The contour is asked for and never arrives

The block says "a confident dark contour" and no generation has returned one. Every form reads as one flat colour directly against another, which is what the specimens above are and what the surface has been designed around. So the colour-against-colour look is emergent rather than specified, which is worth knowing because it means the phrase is not what earns it. The phrase is part of the block all the same: both reference generations carried it, the flatness it steers towards is worth more than the contour it fails to buy, and the block goes out as it stands.

The name is written straight onto the desk, in light type carried by a soft shadow. It is the macOS desktop treatment in this surface's colours. The prompt notice shares this treatment because it is the only other product text read directly from the wallpaper.

Where those three numbers come from

Apple does not publish its values. KDE does, and it solves the same problem on the same kind of ground: FolderItemDelegate.qml puts a DropShadow under the desktop label at offset 1/1, blur radius 2, spread 0.15, in black. The shipped technique is a tight shadow, barely more than a displacement, and not a glow.

Offset and blur fail in opposite directions. A small offset keeps its alpha undiluted but protects only the glyph edges it happens to fall on; a large blur reaches every edge and dilutes itself into nothing. What works is a small offset plus just enough blur to reach the far side of the stem, and then stacking rather than deepening. That is the table above.

Three grounds decided it, because the desk is one photograph and a capability can be dragged anywhere on it: the pale sky the logo grid stands in, the mid-green meadow below, and the dark foliage on the right. Light type over light ground is the one stated failure mode, and this wallpaper walks into it, since the grid sits in the upper left.

The plate holds everywhere because it brings its own ground, and that is where the surface started. It is also four more rectangles on a desk that already has four pictures standing on it. Bare ink fails outright on foliage. The inverse, ink type over a light shadow, held on all three and was the safer choice on paper, and it was dropped anyway, because it is not the treatment this desk is quoting. What the settled one buys with its deep third pass is sky, which is the weakest of its three grounds and the only one that matters day to day. Sky is the ground to re-check after any change to the wallpaper or to the shadow.

A shadow, not an outline

An outline rings every glyph in hard ink and reads as a sticker. A shadow sits under the type, mostly below it, and is never the thing you look at. This is easy to get wrong in one particular way: reaching for stacked hard offsets because the surface bans blur.

The no-blur rule does not reach here. It governs the shadow an object casts onto the desk, where a soft edge would fake depth the flat-band model does not have. Type read straight off a photograph is a different problem, and one this surface had not met before, because until now a name always sat on paper. These three passes are Aluna's only blurred type treatment.

Why three passes and not one

Stacking lets the shadow go this dark without the type looking embossed. One shadow at the same total alpha starts to separate from its letters; three at graded blur do not. It is also why the type had to grow: 10.5px was a size the plate could choose, because paper does the separating for it. Read off a photograph it cannot, and 12px is where macOS sets a desktop label for the same reason.

The seed and the artwork are both stored. A logo is a capability's permanent identity, so the returned SVG is kept exactly as it arrived and the call is never repeated to redraw one. Evolving the capability does not reach the logo; only deleting the capability and growing it again produces a different one. The artwork is a file in the capability's own directory and the seed is a value beside it in the registry, kept as the record of what drew it and never derived from the capability's name or position — the rule every seed on this surface keeps (D11).

Every number above was measured off generations that were made, and the failures decided as much as the keepers did. So what each one was worth is written here and the pictures are gone. The earlier sweeps went the same way: substyle registers, uniform desks, abstract marks and local raster models were all dead ends.

Why two colours, and why the ground is pinned

The construction was found on a pair of generations, both recraftv3_vector at seed 41276 and alike in everything but their colour handling. Two colours with the ground pinned to one of them returned a simple form on a single flat field, 16 kB of SVG. The whole palette passed instead returned a busier composition at 111 kB, and gave the model enough colour to build a horizon out of. Same model, same substyle, same seed, so the colour count is the lever, which is where the two-colour rule comes from. Passing many colours without pinning background_color got a ground blocked into quadrants, so the list is the fault and the control is not.

Eight generations settled it on 2026-08-07: four capabilities made twice, holding model, substyle, size, seed and no_text, with the subject phrase fixed per capability so that colour handling was the only difference. The two-colour construction won and the other was dropped. There is one construction now, and one prompt. The block under the prompt is sent as it stands, the request around it does not vary, and what a caller fills in is a subject and two colours. Nothing else on this page is a knob.

Why a local model never enters the path

Free local rigs were used to refine wording before spending credits, and they cannot judge this style. A general-purpose raster model returned detailed illustration across eight different constructions and ignored the palette every time: the flatness in the keepers was enforced by Recraft's vector substyle, not prompted into existence. So local raster can help decide what to draw and in what colours, and cannot tell you whether the register is right. A local model that emits SVG directly does exist and works, at around 45 seconds a generation on an M4 Max, which is still minutes per capability in a path that has none to spare.

Licences bind even the refinement rig. FLUX.1-dev is non-commercial, so nothing it touches may ship. Qwen-Image, Z-Image Turbo and SDXL are commercially clean. OmniSVG's weights are Apache 2.0 but its training set is CC-BY-NC-SA, which would have to be settled before any output of it were used.

# Decision
L1 Logos come from a hosted service, once per capability. No local model in the creation path. Local rigs are for refining this prompt for free and never produce a logo that ships
L2 Two colours, and the ground is pinned in both places. controls.colors carries exactly two, background_color is the first of them, and the prompt names that colour in words. The second is the companion, which the model names alongside the ground. It was the ground's fixed partner from a closed four-pair lookup until 2026-08-25 — a cap of four distinct pairs for the whole product, invisible against four hand-picked specimens and certain to repeat on a desk of five
L3 Generation decides a subject and two colours. Nothing else. Every other property of a logo is applied by code that does not vary. Each colour names one of the eight hue families, so validating one is checking a word against a list, and the seed decides which of that family's four shades is drawn; the subject comes from what the capability is for, and a user asking for a particular drawing is refused by the intent classifier, like any other attempt to steer presentation
L4 The tile has no edge and a 10% rounded corner. The artwork's colour field is the silhouette. This is the surface's only rounded corner and the only exception to D11
L5 The name is set on the desk, not on paper. Outfit 600 at 12px in surface, over three straight-down shadow passes. No plate
L6 That shadow is the only blurred thing in Aluna, and it is a shadow, not an outline. The hard-shadow rule governs objects casting onto the desk, which a glyph does not do
L7 A logo is made once and never remade. It is stored with its seed and used unchanged for the whole life of that capability. Rendering does not regenerate it and evolving the capability does not touch it — v2, v3 and vn all keep the drawing v1 was born with. Deleting the capability and creating it again is the only route to a different logo
L8 Whatever comes back is used as it stands. The returned SVG is stored as received, gradients and all: no stripping, no path rewriting, no cleanup, no retouching. Everything the shell adds sits outside the file — the clip, the shadow and the size
L9 Two capabilities are allowed to look alike. The palette is small, so a shared ground or a repeated subject is not a defect, and nothing in the request spends effort keeping two logos apart
L10 The logo is the last step, after the gate. The request is made once the build has cleared everything that could still refuse it, and never before. A build that fails, is refused as stale, or is cancelled costs nothing, because nothing was ordered. The consequence is that a capability exists on the desk for a moment before it has a face: until the artwork lands its tile is a placeholder, which is also what marks the ground while the build is still running (D6)
L11 A logo that fails to arrive is retried, never faked. Offline, rate-limited or down, the capability is finished and usable and keeps the placeholder; the request is retried later and the artwork swaps in when it succeeds. Nothing substitutes a drawn stand-in for a logo, because a stand-in that persists becomes the capability's face by accident — and L7 would then forbid replacing it. Loading the desk is the retry: it sweeps every faceless capability once, which needs no scheduler, and stops for good after three attempts, after which the placeholder is permanent. At $0.08 a call the expensive failure is a loop, not a few extra tries
L12 The artwork is a file, served from its own address. It sits in the capability's artifacts directory rather than in a registry column, so it dies with the capability for free and a registry read stops carrying a picture nobody asked for. The route serves one capability's logo as image/svg+xml, marked immutable — which L7 is what makes safe, since a logo is never remade — and carrying the headers that make it picture-only, so opening its address renders an image and runs nothing. The stored bytes are never touched, which is L8 kept literally. The provenance manifest is a flat 4.4 kB and is kept; the response is compressed instead, which recovers more than stripping it would

Nothing about a logo is undecided. What was open is kept here with the reason it closed, so that a later reader does not reopen it. Three of these closed as decisions rather than as fixes: the thing described is real, it is allowed, and no code goes looking for it.

Was open How it closed
The desk still draws the other logo (closed) The desk now stands its capabilities on these four generations, under the tile and label rules in styles/components/logo-contract.css, which the desk page loads from the same stylesheet manifest. The shell-drawn landform tile (a sky band, two coloured horizons, and a black-and-white subject fitted to a 32px box) is gone from the tree: its renderer, its geometry and drawing modules, its frozen subject set and the subject prompt that fed it have all been deleted, along with the two ink helpers that existed only to draw it. There is one logo system now, and this page is it
Two subjects colliding on one desk (closed) Accepted, and not worth engineering against. The palette is small, so two capabilities sharing a ground is going to happen however hard the request works, and a repeated subject is no worse.
